  \note{FunSearch uses a frozen pretrained Codey model: learning occurs in the
    population of programs and the examples selected for prompts, not through
    updating model weights. Code changes means generating a new version of the
    function being evolved, rather than necessarily emitting a patch.
    Diagram colours distinguish orange human specification, turquoise LLM code
    proposals, grey fixed evaluation code and its results, and purple stored
    programs reused as examples. The evaluator is a computer program, not an LLM.
    The human supplies the evaluation function, an initial
    program, and usually a skeleton whose critical function is evolved.
    Invalid candidates are rejected; valid programs and their scores enter a
    diverse population database. The output is the best program found so far.
    This editable workflow is a simplified redrawing of Figure 1 of the paper.
    The cap-set illustration selects four points in the two-dimensional finite
    affine plane over F3; it explains the constraint but is not the discovered
    eight-dimensional 512-point construction. Lines are interpreted over F3,
    including wrap-around, and only distinct triples are forbidden.
    The neighbouring forbidden example selects the three points of one row,
    a full affine line in F3 squared; a red diagonal strike-through marks its
    violation of the cap-set condition. Pale grey points are not selected.
    The 512-point construction improved the previously known size of 496.
    It does not establish that 512 is maximal or solve the general cap-set problem.
    The paper also improves asymptotic lower bounds through admissible sets.
    The packing illustration uses four items of sizes .4, .4, .6, .6 and bins
    of capacity one. First fit in that order uses three bins; a suitable packing
    uses two. This is an explanatory example, not a recorded FunSearch output.
    Actual reported bin-packing results compare evolved online heuristics with
    first fit and best fit on OR-Library and Weibull benchmarks (Table 1).
    Online means that each item must be assigned when it arrives, without
    knowing future items, and that assignments cannot subsequently be changed.
    Empirical improvements do not establish universal superiority or optimality.
    AlphaEvolve is a later evolutionary coding agent powered by Gemini models,
    rather than a standalone model or a claim that it specifically improved
    this displayed bin-packing example. Its white paper describes it as a
    substantial enhancement of FunSearch (Section 1, Table 1).
    Its search procedure found a rank-48 bilinear algorithm for multiplying
    two 4-by-4 complex matrices, compared with rank 49 from recursive Strassen.
    This counts scalar multiplications and is not a general wall-clock
    speedup claim, a proof of optimality, or an improved asymptotic exponent.
    The white paper distinguishes this tensor-decomposition result from
    non-recursive methods using fewer multiplications (Section 3.1, footnote 3).
    Checked against the local AlphaEvolve white paper and DeepMind's report
    dated 14 May 2025; arXiv:2506.13131.
    Sources and reading notes: references/history_papers/.

\begin{frame}[t]{AlphaProof\quad{\fontsize{14}{17}\selectfont\mdseries%
  \href{https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-025-09833-y}{\color{teal}Hubert et al., \emph{Nature} (2026)}}}
  \fontsize{9}{11}\selectfont
  \noindent{\color{muted}Another way to verify and evaluate mathematics is through formal verification in Lean.\par}
  \vspace{0mm}
  \noindent\makebox[\textwidth][c]{%
    \includegraphics[clip,width=\textwidth,height=36mm,keepaspectratio]{alphaproof_architecture.pdf}}\par
  \vspace{1mm}
  \noindent\textbf{Search:} the LLM proposes tactics; Lean returns new proof states.\par
  \vspace{1mm}
  \noindent\textbf{Learning:} RL updates the network. Hard targets get further training on related variants.\par
  \vspace{1.5mm}
  \noindent{\color{teal}\textbf{IMO 2024: silver-medal level --- 28/42 points with AlphaGeometry 2.}}\par
  \noindent AlphaProof solved 3 problems; AlphaGeometry 2 solved 1.\par
  \vspace{0mm}
  {\fontsize{7}{8.5}\selectfont\color{muted}%
    Adapted from Figs.~1--2 and the AlphaProof section.
    \href{https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-025-09833-y}{\color{teal}\emph{Nature} \textbf{651}, 607--613 (2026).}\par}
  \note{Architecture recap, not a claim that a general chat model simply calls Lean.
    The proof network is a specialized three-billion-parameter encoder-decoder
    transformer. It predicts a policy over tactics and a value estimating proof
    difficulty. An AND--OR tree search explores tactic sequences and handles
    decomposition into subgoals. Lean executes tactics, updates hypotheses and
    goals, and its kernel checks completed proof terms.
    Orange denotes the formalized task, turquoise the neural model and its
    proposals/updates, grey programmed search and Lean execution, and purple
    feedback or recorded experience. The lower loop summarizes RL training;
    it is not a weight update after every tactic during ordinary inference.
    Training includes pretraining, supervised learning from Mathlib proofs,
    then reinforcement learning on a large auto-formalized curriculum.
    The simplified diagram shows successful, formally verified outcomes feeding
    training; the full method also handles failed and timed-out search attempts.
    At inference, larger tree-search budgets explore more possibilities. For hard
    targets, test-time RL trains on generated related variants before renewed
    proof attempts. Ordinary search and test-time RL are distinct mechanisms.
    Lean checks the formal proposition; humans must ensure it represents the
    intended mathematical question. IMO 2024 statements were formalized by experts.
    AlphaProof solved P1, P2 and P6, including the hardest problem, P6, solved
    by only five human contestants. AlphaGeometry 2 solved P4. Their combined
    score was 28/42, equivalent to silver-medal level, not an official medal.
    The comparison is by score: AlphaProof used 2--3 days of test-time RL per
    solved problem, exceeding the human competition time limits. See Fig.~5
    and the IMO 2024 evaluation section of the paper.
    Citation uses the published journal volume year, 2026; online publication was
    12 November 2025, and the IMO demonstration was in July 2024.
    Source PDF: references/history_papers/alphaproof_2025.pdf, pp. 2--3.
    Editable illustration: assets/alphaproof_architecture.drawio.}
\end{frame}

      {\color{teal}\bfseries Environment}\par

      \vspace{1.5mm}

      {\color{teal}\bfseries Context}\par
      The information given to the LLM now:\par
      instructions, messages and tool results.\par
      \vspace{1.5mm}

      {\color{teal}\bfseries Tool}\par
      An interface to observe or change the
      environment, e.g. search or Python.\par
      \vspace{1.5mm}

      {\color{teal}\bfseries Agent}\par
      An LLM-based system that uses tools
      and chooses its next step from results.\par
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
  \vspace{1mm}
  {\fontsize{7}{8.5}\selectfont\color{muted}%
    Based on
    \href{https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/agents-api/architecture}{\color{teal}OpenAI's Codex harness documentation}
    and \href{https://code.claude.com/docs/en/how-claude-code-works}{\color{teal}Claude Code's agentic loop}.\par}
  \note{Working definitions for this talk. The agent is the overall system:
    a model, instructions, context management, tools and a control loop.
    A tool is the interface through which it observes or changes its environment.
    For example, a Python execution tool runs code in a workspace and returns
    output; a file-reading tool returns selected file contents.

\begin{frame}[t]{Heuristics for agentic research}
  \noindent{\fontsize{9}{10.7}\selectfont
  \setlength{\tabcolsep}{0pt}
  \renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1}
  \begin{tabular}{@{}p{0.42\textwidth}@{\hspace{4mm}}p{0.54\textwidth}@{}}
    {\color{teal}\bfseries Heuristic} & {\color{chartorange}\bfseries Intuition} \\
    \midrule
    \raggedright\textbf{Ask for a proof or a counterexample.} {\color{teal}[1]}
      & \raggedright Let the agent show that the claim is false; do not let it silently change the problem to obtain a proof.\tabularnewline[1.6mm]
    \raggedright\textbf{Delegate precise mathematical obligations.} {\color{teal}[2]}
      & \raggedright Explicit assumptions and dependencies make partial progress and remaining gaps visible.\tabularnewline[1.6mm]
    \raggedright\textbf{Explore distinct approaches independently.} {\color{teal}[3]}
      & \raggedright Different starting points can reveal approaches that others miss.\tabularnewline[1.6mm]
    \raggedright\textbf{Preserve each claim's status and evidence.} {\color{teal}[4]}
      & \raggedright Reuse must not turn a conjecture or rejected argument into an ``established lemma''.\tabularnewline[1.6mm]
    \raggedright\textbf{Fix identified errors; stop if no progress is made.} {\color{teal}[5]}
      & \raggedright Check that each error was repaired. Repeated approval is not evidence of a correct proof.\tabularnewline
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}\par}
  \vspace{1mm}
  {\fontsize{7}{8.3}\selectfont\color{muted}
    Evidence-informed design heuristics: support ranges from benchmark experiments
    to research-system case studies.\par}
  \vspace{1mm}
  {\fontsize{6.8}{8}\selectfont\color{teal}
    \href{https://arxiv.org/html/2510.04721v1}{[1] Petrov et al. (2025)};\quad
    \href{https://arxiv.org/html/2605.22875v1}{[2] Zhao et al. (2026)};\quad
    \href{https://aclanthology.org/2026.findings-acl.1694/}{[3] Zhu et al. (2026)};\par
    \href{https://arxiv.org/html/2610.04829v1}{[4] Xu et al. (2026)};\quad
    \href{https://arxiv.org/html/2605.06651v1}{[5] Zheng et al. (2026)}.\par}
  \note{These five heuristics complement the preceding slide's context and
    communication principles. The slide follows the owner's supplied rule and
    intuition format. They are evidence-informed design choices, not universally
    established optimal policies. Primary sources and cited passages were checked
    on 19 October 2026.

    1. A fixed target prevents apparent success through changing the problem.
    A demand for a proof can encourage defence of a false premise. Permit a
    counterexample, and require any added hypothesis or weakened conclusion to
    be reported explicitly. BrokenMath finds that prompting models to validate
    a statement first reduces sycophancy, but some responses silently solve a
    corrected problem. This motivates premise checking together with tracking
    changes to the target.
    Reference: Ivo Petrov, Jasper Dekoninck and Martin Vechev (2025),
    \emph{BrokenMath: A Benchmark for Sycophancy in Theorem Proving with LLMs},
    arXiv:2510.04721v1, Section 5.1.
    \url{https://arxiv.org/html/2510.04721v1}.

    2. Precise obligations make coordination and checking easier. Assigning a
    bound under explicit hypotheses exposes what another agent may assume and
    which later steps depend on the result. Let the decomposition evolve with
    the mathematics. RMA's ablations support problem analysis, assumption
    checking and structured knowledge within its workflow. Its evaluation uses
    ten First Proof problems and expert comparisons between submissions. The
    exact obligation-based task format is our implementation inference, not a
    separately isolated experimental result.
    Reference: Zelin Zhao, Bo Yuan, Jaemoo Choi and Yongxin Chen (2026),
    \emph{RMA: an Agentic System for Research-Level Mathematical Problems},
    arXiv:2605.22875v1, Table 3 and Section 4.3.
    \url{https://arxiv.org/html/2605.22875v1}.

    3. Early diversity protects against premature convergence. Agents that
    immediately read the same proposed proof may inherit the same central idea
    and error. Independent initial attempts preserve alternative constructions
    or reductions before selecting a direction. Zhu et al. study diverse
    candidate selection and calibrated confidence communication across six
    reasoning-oriented question-answering benchmarks. Extending this to
    independent research approaches is a plausible design inference, not a
    directly established research-productivity result.
    Reference: Xiaochen Zhu, Caiqi Zhang, Yizhou Chi, Tom Stafford, Nigel Collier
    and Andreas Vlachos (2026), \emph{Demystifying Multi-Agent Debate: The Role
    of Confidence and Diversity}, Findings of ACL 2026, pp. 33909--33930,
    DOI: 10.18653/v1/2026.findings-acl.1694.
    \url{https://aclanthology.org/2026.findings-acl.1694/}.

    4. Memory can amplify discoveries and mistakes. A statement from a rejected
    proof may depend on an assumption introduced for contradiction. Preserve
    assumptions, dependencies, evidence, source context and verification status
    when storing it. The Viva La Vida audit documents extracted critique claims
    losing that refutational context. Its 26 percent figure is the share of
    stored lemmas originating from critiques, not an error rate. The lesson is
    to retain why a claim appeared and what established it, if anything.
    Reference: Benji Xu, Ken Zheng and Noah Han (2026), \emph{Viva La Vida:
    Verification and Accumulation Failures in Multi-Agent Proof Search},
    arXiv:2610.04829v1, Section 5.
    \url{https://arxiv.org/html/2610.04829v1}.

    5. Review can optimise an argument for the reviewer without repairing it.
    Require objections to identify exact steps and revisions to supply concrete
    mathematical responses. If a loop produces no new evidence, change the
    checking method or retain the unresolved gap. The AI co-mathematician paper
    reports reviewer-pleasing false consensus and non-terminating review cycles.
    These observed failures motivate the heuristic; they do not establish a
    universally optimal number of rounds or stopping rule.
    Reference: Daniel Zheng et al. (2026), \emph{AI Co-Mathematician:
    Accelerating Mathematicians with Agentic AI}, arXiv:2605.06651v1, Section 7.
    \url{https://arxiv.org/html/2605.06651v1}.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[t]{Step 1: the precise problem}
  \begin{columns}[T,onlytextwidth]
    \begin{column}{0.67\textwidth}
      \raggedright\fontsize{9}{11}\selectfont
      \setlength{\abovedisplayskip}{3pt}\setlength{\belowdisplayskip}{3pt}
      \setlength{\abovedisplayshortskip}{3pt}\setlength{\belowdisplayshortskip}{3pt}
      A \textbf{simple point process} $X$ on $\mathbb R^d$ is a random set of distinct
      points, finite in bounded regions. Its \textbf{count} in $B$ is\par
      \par\vspace{1mm}\noindent\makebox[\linewidth][c]{$\displaystyle X(B)=\#(X\cap B).$}\par\vspace{1mm}
      The \textbf{factorial moment measure} counts distinct, ordered $n$-tuples:\par
      \par\vspace{1mm}\noindent\makebox[\linewidth][c]{$\displaystyle \alpha^{(n)}(B_1\times\cdots\times B_n)
        =\mathbb E\!\sum_{(x_1,\ldots,x_n)\in X^n_{\ne}}
          \prod_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf 1_{B_i}(x_i).$}\par\vspace{1mm}
      At order two,\par
      \par\vspace{1mm}\noindent\makebox[\linewidth][c]{$\begin{aligned}\alpha^{(2)}(B_1\times B_2)
          &=\mathbb E[X(B_1)X(B_2)]\\[-1mm]
          &\quad-\mathbb E[X(B_1\cap B_2)].\end{aligned}$}\par\vspace{1mm}
      \vspace{1mm}
      {\color{teal}\bfseries Interpretation:} the expected number of ordered pairs
      of distinct points, with the first in $B_1$ and the second in $B_2$.\par
      A point is never paired with itself.
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.30\textwidth}
      \centering
      \includegraphics[clip,width=\linewidth,height=50mm,keepaspectratio]{point_process_patterns.pdf}\par
      {\fontsize{7}{8}\selectfont\color{muted}Schematic point patterns.\par}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
  \vspace{1mm}
  {\fontsize{7}{8}\selectfont\color{muted}
    \href{https://spatstat.org/Aalborg2022/notes/lecturesRW.pdf}{\color{teal}Waagepetersen (2022), slides 7--13};
    \href{https://stoch.math.kit.edu/img/Last/lastpenrose2017.pdf}{\color{teal}Last and Penrose, Section 4.4}.\par}
  \note{We identify a simple point process with its random locally finite support
    and write X(B) for the associated counting measure. All B_i are bounded Borel
    sets. X^n_{ne} denotes ordered n-tuples of distinct points, so no factor 1/n!
    is needed. Factorial expectation measures are defined as nonnegative measures.
    The interpretation is an ensemble average of the number of ordered pairs
    with first point in B_1 and second point in B_2, excluding self-pairs.
    The diagonal correction is essential if the sets overlap. In particular,
    alpha^(2)(B x B)=E[X(B)(X(B)-1)]. For disjoint B_1,B_2, the intersection term
    vanishes, giving exactly E[X(B_1)X(B_2)]. A homogeneous Poisson process gives
    equality with the product of means. Clustering or repulsion can yield excess
    or fewer joint counts nearby, but the sign depends on model, regions and scale.
    Both sketches have 24 points in equally sized windows. They are designed
    illustrations, not fitted simulations or statistical estimates: three compact
    groups versus a separated pattern. They illustrate close-pair frequency;
    one cannot infer a process law or covariance from one such configuration.
    Editable source: assets/point_process_patterns.drawio.
    This establishes vocabulary for the upcoming mathematical problem without
    asserting an additional research conjecture.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[t]{Step 1: the precise problem}
  \noindent\begin{minipage}{\textwidth}
  \raggedright\fontsize{9}{10.5}\selectfont
  \setlength{\abovedisplayskip}{4pt}\setlength{\belowdisplayskip}{4pt}
  \setlength{\abovedisplayshortskip}{4pt}\setlength{\belowdisplayshortskip}{4pt}
  When $\alpha^{(n)}$ has a locally integrable density, the \textbf{product density} is defined by\par
  \vspace{0.5mm}\noindent\makebox[\linewidth][c]{$\displaystyle
    \alpha^{(n)}(A)=\int_A\rho^{(n)}(x_1,\ldots,x_n)\,\mathrm dx_1\cdots\mathrm dx_n.$}\par\vspace{0.5mm}
  At first order, $\rho^{(1)}$ is the density (derivative) of the \textbf{mean count}
  $\alpha^{(1)}(B)=\mathbb E[X(B)]$:\par
  \vspace{0.5mm}\noindent\makebox[\linewidth][c]{$\displaystyle
    \rho^{(1)}(x)=\frac{\mathrm d\alpha^{(1)}}{\mathrm dx}(x),\qquad
    \rho^{(2)}(x,y)=\frac{\mathbb E[X(\mathrm dx)X(\mathrm dy)]}{\mathrm dx\,\mathrm dy}\quad(x\ne y).$}\par\vspace{0.5mm}
  With positive intensity densities $\rho^{(1)}(x_i)$, define the \textbf{$n$-point correlation function}:\par
  \par\vspace{0.5mm}\noindent\makebox[\linewidth][c]{$\displaystyle g_n(x_1,\ldots,x_n)
       =\frac{\rho^{(n)}(x_1,\ldots,x_n)}{\prod\nolimits_{i=1}^{n}\rho^{(1)}(x_i)}.$}\par\vspace{0.5mm}
  \textbf{Stationarity:} shifting all points leaves their joint law unchanged:
  $X+t\overset{d}{=}X$ for every $t\in\mathbb R^d$.\par
  Then $\rho^{(1)}(x)=\lambda$ is constant and $g_n=\rho^{(n)}/\lambda^n$ (assume $0<\lambda<\infty$).\par
  \vspace{1mm}
  Relative to Poisson with the same intensity:\par
  \vspace{0.5mm}
  \textbf{$g_n>1$: more such tuples.\qquad $g_n<1$: fewer such tuples.}\par
  \end{minipage}
  \vspace{0.8mm}
  {\fontsize{7}{8.5}\selectfont\color{muted}
    For pairs: clustering or repulsion at the specified locations and scale.
    $g_2=1$ alone does not imply Poisson.\par
    \href{https://spatstat.org/Aalborg2022/notes/lecturesRW.pdf}{\color{teal}Waagepetersen (2022), slides 9--13};
    \href{https://stoch.math.kit.edu/img/Last/lastpenrose2017.pdf}{\color{teal}Last and Penrose, Section 4.4}.\par}
  \note{rho^(n) denotes order n, not the nth power of a scalar rho. It is a
    Radon--Nikodym derivative, defined Lebesgue almost everywhere; local finiteness
    and absolute continuity are sufficient conditions here. The infinitesimal
    formula is notation, not division of ordinary random variables by infinitesimals.
    Precisely, for disjoint B_1,B_2, E[X(B_1)X(B_2)] is the integral of rho^(2)
    over B_1 x B_2. For shrinking disjoint neighbourhoods of distinct x,y, the
    quotient by their volumes converges to rho^(2)(x,y) at suitable differentiation
    points; continuity is a simple sufficient condition. On the diagonal, the
    ordinary second moment measure has the contribution of counting the same
    point twice, which explains the restriction x differs from y.
    Normalisation gives the full product density divided by the product of
    intensity densities, not a factorial cumulant or Pearson coefficient.
    The mean measure alpha^(1)(B)=E[X(B)] is already introduced by the
    factorial moment notation. Its Radon--Nikodym
    derivative with respect to Lebesgue measure is rho^(1); this is the
    measure-theoretic meaning of derivative of the mean function here.
    Stationarity means invariance of the entire point-process law under every
    translation, not merely constancy of the mean. With positive finite intensity,
    alpha^(1)(B)=lambda |B|, so rho^(1)=lambda almost everywhere and
    g_n=rho^(n)/lambda^n. Constant intensity alone does not imply stationarity.
    The higher product densities are invariant under simultaneous translation
    of all their arguments, almost everywhere; they need not be constant in
    the relative positions. The displayed rho^(2) formula is infinitesimal shorthand.
    A Poisson process has factorial moment measures equal to the product of
    its intensity measure, giving g_n=1. Values above or below one compare local
    tuple densities; the comparison need not hold at every scale.
    Higher-order full correlations include lower-order contributions and differ
    from connected correlations. The local manuscript uses m_n for rho^(n),
    rho for lambda, and the same g_n normalisation.}
\end{frame}
