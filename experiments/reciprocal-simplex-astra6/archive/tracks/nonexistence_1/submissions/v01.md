## Exact claim and scope

For every $\lambda_0>0$ and every finite $a_2>0$, there is no simple, locally finite point process on $\mathbb R^3$ having mean measure $\lambda_0\,dx$ and full second factorial expectation measure
\[
 \alpha^{(2)}(dx\,dy)=\frac{a_2}{\|y-x\|}\,dx\,dy.
\]
The identity is understood Lebesgue-almost everywhere off the diagonal, with no singular component. This theorem does not need stationarity beyond the specified mean measure. It does not assume a finite second, third or fourth count moment; the displayed identity itself supplies the local second moment needed in its proof. Consequently S0 is false for each $\lambda_0>0$, regardless of the choices of $a_3,a_4$.

We also prove three complementary statements:

1. For a stationary positive finite-intensity process, even a full pair measure whose density is uniformly bounded by a quantity tending to zero at large separation is impossible. No restriction is placed on its short-range second moment measure. Thus a lower-distance cutoff alone cannot repair the global pair law.
2. The global full triangle law $\rho^{(3)}=a_3/A$, by itself, is incompatible with the same positive constant mean intensity.
3. The full tetrahedron law $\rho^{(4)}=a_4/V$ on every noncoplanar configuration in an open ball forces an infinite fourth count moment in that ball. This excludes the separate finite-fourth-moment subclass, but is not used as a general contradiction to S0.

All constants in these statements may be chosen with the process. Neither isotropy, mixing, ergodicity, a Cox or cluster representation, nor any asymptotic factorisation is assumed.

## Argument

### 1. The global pair law contradicts a count inequality

Write $B_R=B(0,R)$, $v=|B_1|=4\pi/3$, and $N_R=X(B_R)$. For a nonnegative integer $N$ put $(N)_k=N(N-1)\cdots(N-k+1)$, with $(N)_k=0$ when $N<k$.

For a simple locally finite process the number of ordered distinct pairs in $B_R$ is $(N_R)_2$. The assumed full measure identity therefore gives
\[
 \mathbb E(N_R)_2
 =a_2\int_{B_R}\int_{B_R}\frac{dy\,dx}{\|y-x\|}.
 \tag{1}
\]
The diagonal has Lebesgue measure zero. The exclusion of a singular component is what permits (1) to account for the entire factorial expectation.

For each $x\in B_R$, translation of the inner integral and enlargement of its domain give
\[
 \int_{B_R}\frac{dy}{\|y-x\|}
 \leq \int_{\|h\|<2R}\frac{dh}{\|h\|}
 =4\pi\int_0^{2R}r\,dr
 =8\pi R^2.
 \tag{2}
\]
All integrands are nonnegative, so these integrations and domain comparisons are valid by Tonelli's theorem. In particular, (1) is finite:
\[
 \mathbb E(N_R)_2\leq 8\pi a_2vR^5.
 \tag{3}
\]
Since $\mathbb E N_R=\lambda_0vR^3$ and $N_R^2=(N_R)_2+N_R$, the second moment is finite. Nonnegativity of the variance now yields
\[
 \lambda_0^2v^2R^6
 =(\mathbb E N_R)^2
 \leq\mathbb E N_R^2
 \leq 8\pi a_2vR^5+\lambda_0vR^3.
\]
Dividing by $vR^6$ gives, for every $R>0$,
\[
 \lambda_0^2v\leq\frac{8\pi a_2}{R}+\frac{\lambda_0}{R^3}.
 \tag{4}
\]
The right-hand side tends to zero as $R\to\infty$, whereas the left-hand side is positive. This proves the first claim.

This is an expectation argument on deterministic balls. No sample-density limit, ergodic theorem, mixing assertion or convergence to a Poisson process enters it.

### 2. Short-range changes cannot rescue uniform decay of the full pair measure

**Theorem.** Let $X$ be a stationary, simple, locally finite point process on $\mathbb R^3$ with constant intensity $0<\lambda<\infty$. Suppose that for each $\varepsilon>0$ there is a finite $r_\varepsilon$ such that the restriction of $\alpha^{(2)}$ to
\[
 T_\varepsilon=\{(x,y):\|y-x\|>r_\varepsilon\}
\]
is dominated by $\varepsilon$ times six-dimensional Lebesgue measure. There is no such process.

The domination is a measure assertion, so it excludes singular pair mass on the indicated tail. An absolutely continuous tail density with a uniform essential upper bound tending to zero satisfies it. No condition is imposed on $\alpha^{(2)}$ near the diagonal.

**Proof.** Partition space into half-open unit cubes
$Q_z=z+[0,1)^3$, $z\in\mathbb Z^3$. Define
\[
 I_z=\mathbf1\{X(Q_z)>0\},\qquad
 p=\mathbb P(X(Q_0)>0).
\]
Stationarity makes $\mathbb E I_z=p$ for all $z$. Moreover $p>0$, because $\mathbb E X(Q_0)=\lambda>0$; a nonnegative random variable that vanishes almost surely cannot have positive mean.

For $K_m=\{1,\ldots,m\}^3$, let $n=m^3$ and $S_m=\sum_{z\in K_m}I_z$. Then $0\leq S_m\leq n$, $\mathbb E S_m=np$, and
\[
 n^2p^2\leq\mathbb E S_m^2.
 \tag{5}
\]
Fix $\varepsilon>0$ and choose $L>r_\varepsilon+\sqrt3$. For $z\ne w$ with $\|z-w\|>L$, every pair $x\in Q_z$, $y\in Q_w$ has $\|y-x\|>r_\varepsilon$. The cubes are disjoint, and therefore
\[
 \mathbb E(I_zI_w)
 \leq\mathbb E[X(Q_z)X(Q_w)]
 =\alpha^{(2)}(Q_z\times Q_w)
 \leq\varepsilon.
 \tag{6}
\]
The product identity in (6) counts distinct pairs automatically because the cubes are disjoint.

For the remaining pairs use the bound $\mathbb E(I_zI_w)\leq1$. The number of nonzero lattice offsets of norm at most $L$ is a finite constant $C_L$ independent of $m$. Summing over ordered pairs of cubes gives
\[
 \mathbb E S_m^2
 \leq n+C_Ln+\varepsilon n^2.
 \tag{7}
\]
After division by $n^2$, (5)–(7) imply
$p^2\leq(1+C_L)/n+\varepsilon$. First let $m\to\infty$ with $\varepsilon$ fixed, and then let $\varepsilon\downarrow0$. This gives $p=0$, a contradiction. $\square$

In particular, the identity $\rho^{(2)}=a_2/D$ on all $D>r_0$, with finite $r_0,a_2$, satisfies the theorem's tail hypothesis. It remains impossible even if the full pair measure at $D\leq r_0$ is arbitrary and has infinite bounded-set mass.

The theorem does **not** assert $\rho^{(2)}\to\lambda^2$, or pointwise convergence to any limit. It also does not rule out prescribing the reciprocal law on selected unbounded sets of separations while allowing substantial pair mass elsewhere.

### 3. The unrestricted triangle law has its own global obstruction

**Theorem.** For every $\lambda>0$ and finite $a_3>0$, there is no simple locally finite point process with mean measure $\lambda\,dx$ and full third factorial measure having density $a_3/A$ on all noncollinear triples.

**Proof.** For $x_1\in B_R$, set $u=x_2-x_1$ and $w=x_3-x_1$. Both displacement vectors have norm at most $2R$. Since $A=\|u\times w\|/2$, nonnegative integration gives
\[
 \int_{B_R^3}\frac{dx_1\,dx_2\,dx_3}{A(x_1,x_2,x_3)}
 \leq |B_R|
 \int_{\|u\|<2R}\int_{\|w\|<2R}
 \frac{2\,dw\,du}{\|u\times w\|}.
 \tag{8}
\]
For $u\ne0$, use spherical coordinates for $w$ with polar angle $\theta$ relative to $u$. The volume element's $\sin\theta$ cancels the $\sin\theta$ in $\|u\times w\|=\|u\|\|w\|\sin\theta$. Including the direction of $u$, the displacement integral in (8) is
\[
 2(4\pi)(2\pi)
 \left(\int_0^{2R}r\,dr\right)
 \left(\int_0^{2R}s\,ds\right)
 \left(\int_0^\pi d\theta\right)
 =64\pi^3R^4.
 \tag{9}
\]
Coordinate singularities and the collinear set have Lebesgue measure zero. For example, for fixed $x_1\ne x_2$, the allowed collinear values of $x_3$ lie on a line of three-dimensional measure zero; Fubini gives the assertion for triples. Thus (8)–(9) prove local integrability of $1/A$, including near coincidences and collinearity.

The full third measure identity yields
\[
 \mathbb E(N_R)_3\leq64\pi^3a_3vR^7.
 \tag{10}
\]
For every nonnegative integer $N$,
$(N)_3\geq(N-2)_+^3$. The function $t\mapsto(t-2)_+^3$ is convex, so Jensen's inequality gives
\[
 \mathbb E(N_R)_3\geq(\mathbb E N_R-2)_+^3
 =(\lambda vR^3-2)_+^3.
 \tag{11}
\]
The expectation in (10) is finite; Jensen may also be stated for extended nonnegative expectations, so no extra moment hypothesis is being inserted. Dividing (10)–(11) by $R^9$ and letting $R\to\infty$ gives $(\lambda v)^3\leq0$, a contradiction. $\square$

This theorem is independent of the pair-density theorem. Repairing only the pair law while leaving the unrestricted triangle identity intact cannot restore existence.

### 4. The tetrahedron singularity and the separate finite-moment class

**Proposition.** Let $B$ be any nonempty open ball. Suppose the restriction of the full fourth factorial measure to $B^4$ has density $a_4/V$ Lebesgue-almost everywhere on $V>0$, where $0<a_4<\infty$. Then
\[
 \alpha^{(4)}(B^4)=\mathbb E(X(B))_4=\infty,
 \qquad
 \mathbb E X(B)^4=\infty.
 \tag{12}
\]
Stationarity and a prescribed intensity are not needed for this proposition.

**Proof.** Fix a noncollinear triple $x_1,x_2,x_3\in B$ and let $P$ be its affine plane, $A>0$ its area, and $\nu$ a unit normal. Since $x_1$ is interior to $B$, there is $\delta>0$ such that the cylinder
\[
 \{z+t\nu:z\in P,\ \|z-x_1\|<\delta,\ |t|<\delta\}
\]
is contained in $B$. In these orthonormal coordinates $dx_4=d\mathcal H^2(z)\,dt$, and the base-times-height formula gives
\[
 V(x_1,x_2,x_3,z+t\nu)=\frac{A|t|}{3}.
\]
Consequently
\[
 \int_{\substack{x_4\in B\\V>0}}\frac{dx_4}{V}
 \geq\frac{3}{A}
 \int_{\substack{z\in P\\\|z-x_1\|<\delta}}d\mathcal H^2(z)
 \int_{0<|t|<\delta}\frac{dt}{|t|}
 =\infty.
 \tag{13}
\]
Almost every triple in $B^3$ is noncollinear, as established above. Tonelli applied to the nonnegative reciprocal-volume function now gives
$\int_{B^4\cap\{V>0\}}V^{-1}\,d\mathbf x=\infty$. Equality of the full measures and $a_4>0$ yield the first equality in (12). The deterministic inequality $N^4\geq(N)_4$ yields the second. $\square$

This argument integrates only over noncoplanar configurations, $t\ne0$. Changing a density on the exactly coplanar null set cannot remove the divergence. If a replacement retains finite fourth moments, it must alter the reciprocal-volume requirement on enough configurations of positive measure to make the relevant local integrals finite, or otherwise remove that requirement. The proof does not select a particular positive altitude, volume or shape cutoff.

Conversely, (12) alone is compatible with the broader moment conventions of S0. Almost-sure finiteness of a random count and finite first moment do not imply finite fourth moment. For a direct check, a finite integer-valued random variable with
$\mathbb P(N=k)=c\,k^{-5}$ for $k=1,2,\ldots$, where $c$ normalises the probabilities, has finite first, second and third moments and infinite fourth moment. This example concerns count moments only; it is not claimed to realise any spatial factorial density in S0.

## Logical consequences and a proposed replacement question

The decisive general obstruction is global: retaining the full reciprocal pair law at every sufficiently large separation is impossible. The unrestricted triangle law is independently impossible. Some change to these all-scale identities is therefore necessary if positive finite intensity and the full-density interpretation are retained.

A finite upper diameter bound is one possible change, but is not proved necessary. Other possibilities include prescribing the identities only on selected geometric domains with an appropriate complementary region. Changing the large-scale functional forms would also avoid the specific hypotheses proved impossible here, but changes the mathematical target. No such choice is made or adopted by this submission.

For the fourth-order identity, this submission establishes only the stated finite-moment obstruction. It gives no general proof that S0's extended-moment class requires a lower altitude, lower distance or shape cutoff. Imposing one on that basis would exceed the result.

The following is a precise, conservative **replacement question for review**, not an adopted target and not an existence assertion:

> For every prescribed $\lambda_0>0$ and every prescribed finite $M>0$, chosen independently, is there a stationary, simple, locally finite point process on $\mathbb R^3$ with mean measure $\lambda_0\,dx$, constants $a_2,a_3,a_4\in(0,\infty)$, and full factorial expectation measures $\alpha^{(n)}=\rho^{(n)}\,d\mathbf x$, such that
> \[
> \rho^{(n)}(\mathbf x)=a_n/\Delta_n(\mathbf x),
> \qquad n=2,3,4,
> \]
> for Lebesgue-almost every configuration with
> $\operatorname{diam}\{x_1,\ldots,x_n\}<M$ and $\Delta_n>0$, where
> $(\Delta_2,\Delta_3,\Delta_4)=(D,A,V)$?
> No identities are prescribed outside those domains; no singular components are allowed anywhere; bounded-set higher moments may be infinite; and there is no positive lower distance, altitude or shape cutoff.

The constants and the process may depend on both prescribed parameters. This formulation keeps the exact identities, all three orders, prescribed intensity and the extended-moment class; it changes only the upper scale range on which the identities are required. It is not asserted to be sufficient, maximal or uniquely preferred. In particular, the fourth-order local singularity and cross-order compatibility could obstruct it for reasons not proved here. If one instead asks for finite fourth count moments, this candidate remains impossible by Proposition (12), because every sufficiently small open ball has diameter less than $M$.

For fixed $M$, a construction available only below an intensity threshold would not answer the displayed quantifiers. A dilation changes both intensity and the geometric cutoff; it is not by itself a proof for every $\lambda_0$ at a fixed prescribed $M$.
