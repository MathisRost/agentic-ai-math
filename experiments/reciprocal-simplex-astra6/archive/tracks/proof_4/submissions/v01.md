## Exact claim and scope

We prove the following partial result.

**Theorem 1.** For every independently prescribed pair $\lambda_0>0$ and $M>0$, there is a stationary, isotropic, simple, almost surely locally finite point process $X$ on all of $\mathbb R^3$, of intensity exactly $\lambda_0$, whose full factorial expectation measures of orders two, three and four have Lebesgue densities finite almost everywhere globally. There is no singular component. For a positive finite constant $a_4$ depending on $\lambda_0,M$,
\[
 \rho^{(4)}(x_1,x_2,x_3,x_4)=\frac{a_4}{V(x_1,x_2,x_3,x_4)}
 \quad\text{on }E_4(M).
 \tag{1}
\]
The displayed version satisfies this identity at every noncoplanar configuration of diameter below $M$. All bounded-set third count moments are finite, whereas the fourth count moment is infinite in every nonempty open ball.

The same process **does not** satisfy either of S1's reciprocal-distance and reciprocal-area identities. We calculate its full lower-order densities explicitly. We also prove that changing the radial mark distribution and finite slab amplitude within a stated class of this construction cannot repair the pair identity on every sufficiently small positive distance.

Thus the exact unresolved target remains the simultaneous identities at all three orders. Theorem 1 omits two of them and is only partial progress. No target amendment is requested or inferred. The all-intensity and independent-$M$ quantifiers, whole-space process requirements and unrestricted nondegenerate fourth-order shape domain are retained in the partial theorem.

## Argument

### 1. The selected method

We choose a single random family of parallel slabs throughout space and then sample a Poisson process conditional on its random intensity field. The common orientation, spacing and phase are sampled once for the whole process. This dependence differs from independently superposing random carriers.

The slab separation exceeds $M$. Therefore, if several points of diameter below $M$ all have positive conditional intensity, they must lie in the same slab. This eliminates the other slab allocations by a geometric exclusion, while still calculating the full factorial measure. Integrating the four affine slab coordinates then gives an inverse determinant. We retain the positive slab thickness; there is no point mass on any plane.

### 2. An actual whole-space probability law

Fix $\lambda_0,M>0$ and choose $L=2M$. In fixed Euclidean coordinate units define
\[
 I=[-1/2,1/2),\qquad h=\mathbf1_I,\qquad
 A(v)=1+|v|^2,\qquad T(v)=2+L|v|,
 \quad v\in\mathbb R^3.
\]
The symbol $A(v)$ in this subsection is an amplitude function; triangle area will always be written $A_\triangle$ below. Define the finite positive integrals
\[
 Z=\int_{\mathbb R^3}\frac{T(v)}{A(v)^4}\,dv,
 \qquad J=\int_{\mathbb R^3}A(v)^{-3}\,dv.
 \tag{2}
\]
Polar integration gives
\[
 J=\frac{\pi^2}{4},\qquad
 Z=\frac{\pi^2}{4}+\frac{\pi L}{3}.
 \tag{3}
\]
For example, the radial integrals needed for $Z$ are
$\int_0^\infty r^2(1+r^2)^{-4}dr=\pi/32$ and
$\int_0^\infty r^3(1+r^2)^{-4}dr=1/12$.
Finiteness also follows directly: the radial integrand in $Z$ is bounded near zero and is $O(r^{-5})$ at infinity.

Sample a random vector $W$ with probability density
\[
 p(v)=\frac{T(v)}{Z A(v)^4}.
 \tag{4}
\]
Conditional on $W=v$, sample $B$ uniformly on $[0,T(v))$. Put
\[
 s=\frac{\lambda_0 Z}{J}>0,
 \qquad
 \Lambda(x)=s A(W)\sum_{k\in\mathbb Z}
             h(W\cdot x-B-kT(W)).
 \tag{5}
\]
Since $T\geq2$ and $h$ has support of length one, at most one summand in (5) is nonzero at any $x$. Thus $0\leq\Lambda(x)\leq sA(W)<\infty$ for every realised mark. The field is jointly measurable, being a countable sum of measurable functions.

Take a Poisson random measure on $\mathbb R^3\times[0,\infty)$ with Lebesgue intensity, independent of $(W,B)$, and retain a spatial point $x$ when its height coordinate is at most $\Lambda(x)$. Its projection defines $X$. Conditional on $(W,B)$, this is the Poisson process of diffuse locally finite intensity measure $\Lambda(x)\,dx$. This gives a measurable probability law on the entire space, rather than merely a hierarchy of proposed moments.

For every bounded $C$, its conditional mean is at most $sA(W)|C|<\infty$. Conditional Poisson counts are therefore finite almost surely. A countable covering by bounded cubes gives almost-sure local finiteness simultaneously on all bounded sets. The conditional intensity measure is diffuse, so conditional Poisson points are almost surely distinct; again a countable cube covering proves global simplicity.

Translating space by $t$ replaces the phase $B$ by $B-W\cdot t$ modulo $T(W)$. The uniform phase law is invariant under this change, conditional on $W$. Hence the field law and the point-process law are translation invariant. The density of $W$, its amplitude and its period depend only on $|W|$, so rotations also preserve the law. Neither mixing nor ergodicity is claimed or required.

Averaging the phase gives, for each fixed $v,x$,
\[
 \frac1{T(v)}\int_0^{T(v)}
    \sum_k h(v\cdot x-b-kT(v))\,db=\frac1{T(v)}.
\]
Consequently Tonelli and (2)–(5) give
\[
 \mathbb E\Lambda(x)
 =\frac{s}{Z}\int A(v)^{-3}\,dv
 =\frac{sJ}{Z}=\lambda_0.
 \tag{6}
\]
Integrating (6) over $C$ proves $\mathbb EX(C)=\lambda_0|C|$. The choice of $s$ works for every positive $\lambda_0$ at the already fixed $M$; no scaling or small-intensity argument is involved.

### 3. Full factorial measures and every slab allocation

For the conditional Poisson law and every nonnegative measurable test $f$,
\[
 \mathbb E\left[\sum_{x_1,\ldots,x_n\in X}^{\ne}
       f(x_1,\ldots,x_n)\,\middle|\,W,B\right]
 =\int f(\mathbf x)\prod_{i=1}^n\Lambda(x_i)\,d\mathbf x.
 \tag{7}
\]
For completeness, the conditional identity follows first for products of disjoint bounded sets from independent Poisson counts, and for repeated sets from $\mathbb E(N)_q=\mu^q$ for a Poisson count of mean $\mu$. Finite partitions of bounded sets and monotone extension give (7) for nonnegative tests. Its use does not require unconditional moments to be finite.

Tonelli applied to (7) shows that the **full** factorial expectation measure has density
\[
 \rho^{(n)}(\mathbf x)=\mathbb E\prod_{i=1}^n\Lambda(x_i).
 \tag{8}
\]
In particular, there is no omitted singular component anywhere. The argument below establishes finite-almost-everywhere densities at the required orders; this property is not assumed in (8).

Write $u_i=x_i-x_1$ for $i\geq2$. Expanding the nonnegative slab sums in (8), and combining the first slab index with the phase integral, gives
\[
\begin{aligned}
 \rho^{(n)}(\mathbf x)
 ={}&\frac{s^n}{Z}\sum_{m\in\mathbb Z^{n-1}}
 \int_{\mathbb R^3}\int_{\mathbb R}
 A(v)^{n-4}h(-b)\\
 &\hspace{20mm}\times
 \prod_{i=2}^n h(v\cdot u_i-b-m_iT(v))\,db\,dv .
\end{aligned}
 \tag{9}
\]
Here $m_i$ is the difference between the slab index for $x_i$ and the index for $x_1$. To see the change exactly, in each summand set
$b'=b+k_1T(v)-v\cdot x_1$; summing $k_1\in\mathbb Z$ fills $\mathbb R$ by adjacent phase intervals. Rename $b'$ as $b$. The factor $p(v)/T(v)$ is $1/(ZA(v)^4)$. All sums and integrals are nonnegative, so this manipulation is valid even before finiteness is known.

Formula (9) includes every allocation to all slabs. It is not a factorial-cumulant or a selected within-carrier formula.

If $\operatorname{diam}(\mathbf x)<M$ and the integrand of a term in (9) is nonzero, the first and $i$th arguments of $h$ differ by less than one. Therefore
\[
 |v\cdot u_i-m_iT(v)|<1.
 \tag{10}
\]
For $m_i\ne0$ the left side is at least
\[
 T(v)-|v|\,|u_i|>2+(L-M)|v|\geq2,
\]
which contradicts (10). Thus only $m=0$ contributes in this domain, and (9) reduces to
\[
 \rho^{(n)}(\mathbf x)=\frac{s^n}{Z}
 \int_{\mathbb R^3}\int_{\mathbb R}
 A(v)^{n-4}\prod_{i=1}^n h(v\cdot x_i-b)\,db\,dv,
 \quad \operatorname{diam}(\mathbf x)<M.
 \tag{11}
\]
The translation from the anchored to the displayed unanchored form just shifts $b$.

### 4. Exact full fourth density on every required shape

For four affinely independent points, the linear map
\[
 (v,b)\longmapsto(v\cdot x_1-b,\ldots,v\cdot x_4-b)
\]
has determinant of absolute value
\[
 |\det[x_2-x_1,x_3-x_1,x_4-x_1]|=6V(\mathbf x).
\]
At $n=4$ the amplitude weight in (11) is one. The change-of-variables formula therefore yields
\[
 \rho^{(4)}(\mathbf x)
 =\frac{s^4}{6ZV(\mathbf x)}
   \int_{\mathbb R^4}\prod_{i=1}^4h(t_i)\,d\mathbf t
 =\frac{s^4}{6ZV(\mathbf x)}.
 \tag{12}
\]
The integral equals one because $|I|=1$. Thus Theorem 1 has
\[
 a_4=\frac{s^4}{6Z}\in(0,\infty).
\]
No lower edge, area, volume, altitude or shape cutoff was used. Equation (12) includes arbitrarily small and arbitrarily flat nondegenerate tetrahedra with diameter below $M$.

### 5. Global fourth density is finite almost everywhere

The finite-diameter calculation alone would not establish S1's retained global regularity. We now examine all terms in (9), including configurations of diameter at least $M$.

Fix an affinely independent quadruple, and let $U$ be the invertible $3\times3$ matrix with rows $u_2^\top,u_3^\top,u_4^\top$. Put $R=\max_i|u_i|$ and $r=|v|$. Condition (10) implies
\[
 |m_i|\leq\frac{rR+1}{Lr+2}
 \leq\max\{R/L,1/2\}.
 \tag{13}
\]
Only finitely many integer vectors $m$ can therefore contribute for this fixed quadruple.

For one such $m$, (10) gives
\[
 |Uv-m(Lr+2)|\leq\sqrt3.
\]
Writing $w=U^{-1}m$, it follows that
\[
 |v-Lr w|\leq 2|w|+\sqrt3\,\|U^{-1}\|=:K(U,m).
 \tag{14}
\]
By the reverse triangle inequality,
\[
 r\,\big|1-L|w|\big|\leq K(U,m).
 \tag{15}
\]
Unless $L|U^{-1}m|=1$, (15) bounds $|v|$ on the support of this term. Also $h(-b)$ restricts $b$ to an interval of length one. At order four the integrand is bounded by one, so the term has a finite integral. Equation (13) makes the entire sum finite.

The exceptional configurations in this argument form a Lebesgue-null set. Affine dependence is the zero set of the nonzero polynomial $\det U$. For each fixed nonzero integer vector $m$, the equation $L|U^{-1}m|=1$, on invertible matrices, is equivalent to
\[
 L^2|\operatorname{adj}(U)m|^2-(\det U)^2=0.
 \tag{16}
\]
This polynomial is not identically zero: at $U=tI_3$ it is
$L^2t^4|m|^2-t^6$. A nonzero polynomial has a Lebesgue-null zero set, as follows by induction on the number of variables and one-variable root finiteness, using Fubini for the exceptional coefficient sets. The countable union over integer $m\ne0$ remains null. For $m=0$, the exceptional equality cannot occur. The transformation from $(x_1,x_2,x_3,x_4)$ to $(x_1,U)$ is an invertible linear transformation with constant nonzero Jacobian, so these null-set assertions apply to the original configuration space.

Consequently (9) is finite at almost every quadruple, globally. On the null exceptional set its values may be assigned zero without changing the measure. This creates no singular mass: full absolute continuity has already been proved in (7)–(8), including on exactly coplanar configurations and on the exceptional sets in (16).

### 6. Finite third moments and infinite fourth moments

Let $C_R=B(0,R)$, $R>0$, and let
$\mu_R=\int_{C_R}\Lambda(x)\,dx$ be its conditional Poisson mean.
For $r=|W|\leq1$, (5) gives $\mu_R\leq2s|C_R|$.
For $r>1$, a slab can meet $C_R$ only if its centre in the affine coordinate lies in an interval of length $2Rr+1$. The number of possible slab centres is at most
\[
 \frac{2Rr+1}{T(W)}+1\leq\frac{2R}{L}+2.
\]
Increasing this last constant by one, if desired, covers all endpoint conventions. Each slab has physical width $1/r$, and its intersection with the ball has volume at most $\pi R^2/r$ by slicing perpendicular to $W$. Hence
\[
 \mu_R\leq C_R' s\,\frac{1+r^2}{r}
 \leq 2C_R' s r\quad(r>1),
\]
where $C_R'<\infty$ depends on $R,L$. Together the two regimes give
\[
 \mu_R\leq C_R''s(1+r).
 \tag{17}
\]
Under (4), the radial probability density is
$4\pi r^2(2+Lr)/(Z(1+r^2)^4)=O(r^{-5})$ at infinity. Thus $\mathbb E(1+|W|)^3<\infty$. Equation (17) gives $\mathbb E\mu_R^3<\infty$, and the conditional Poisson identity
\[
 \mathbb E[X(C_R)^3\mid W,B]=\mu_R^3+3\mu_R^2+\mu_R
\]
proves finite third count moment. Every bounded Borel set is contained in some $C_R$, so the conclusion is global. It also proves finite first and second moments.

In particular, the densities in (8) at orders two and three are integrable on every bounded product cube and hence finite almost everywhere. Countable exhaustion gives their global finite-almost-everywhere versions. This complements the separate fourth-order argument in Section 5.

Every nonempty open ball contains a smaller ball of diameter below $M$. In that smaller ball, take any noncollinear base triple with area $A_\triangle>0$ and write the fourth vertex as $z+t\nu$, where $z$ is in its affine base plane and $\nu$ is a unit normal. A sufficiently small planar disc about the first vertex, times $|t|<\delta$, lies inside the ball. Since
\[
 V=A_\triangle|t|/3,
\]
the fourth-vertex integral of (12) contains a positive multiple of
$\int_{0<|t|<\delta}|t|^{-1}dt=\infty$. Almost every base triple is noncollinear; Tonelli gives an infinite fourth factorial moment and therefore an infinite fourth raw count moment. Count monotonicity transfers this to the original ball. This does not contradict (17), whose bound has infinite fourth expectation under the mark law.

### 7. The full lower-order densities, and their failure

Let $d=|x_2-x_1|\in(0,M)$. In (11), change variables to $t_1=-b$ and $t_2=v\cdot(x_2-x_1)-b$, leaving the two components of $v$ perpendicular to $x_2-x_1$ free. The Jacobian factor is $1/d$. The integral of $(c+|z|^2)^{-2}$ over $z\in\mathbb R^2$ is $\pi/c$. Consequently
\[
 \rho^{(2)}(d)
 =\frac{\pi s^2}{Zd}\,\Phi(d),\qquad
 \Phi(d)=\int_{I^2}
 \frac{dt_1dt_2}{1+(t_2-t_1)^2/d^2}.
 \tag{18}
\]
The difference of two variables integrated uniformly over $I$ has density $1-|t|$ on $[-1,1]$. Therefore
\[
 \begin{aligned}
 \Phi(d)
 &=2\int_0^1\frac{(1-t)d^2}{d^2+t^2}\,dt\\
 &=2d\arctan(1/d)-d^2\log(1+d^{-2}).
 \end{aligned}
 \tag{19}
\]
In particular,
\[
 \lim_{d\downarrow0}\rho^{(2)}(d)=\frac{\pi^2s^2}{Z}<\infty,
 \qquad
 \lim_{d\downarrow0}d\rho^{(2)}(d)=0.
 \tag{20}
\]
Thus no positive constant $a_2$ can give $\rho^{(2)}=a_2/d$ almost everywhere on $(0,M)$. The formula is continuous there, so almost-everywhere equality would imply pointwise equality and contradict (20).

There is also an explicit third-order discrepancy. Translate $x_1$ to zero, write $u=x_2-x_1$, $w=x_3-x_1$, and assume $A_\triangle=|u\times w|/2>0$. For $\mathbf t\in I^3$ let $v_0(\mathbf t;u,w)$ be the unique vector in $\operatorname{span}\{u,w\}$ satisfying
\[
 v_0\cdot u=t_2-t_1,\qquad
 v_0\cdot w=t_3-t_1.
\]
With $v=v_0+z\nu$, $\nu$ a unit normal to this span, the change of variables has Jacobian $1/|u\times w|$. Also
$\int_{\mathbb R}(1+|v_0|^2+z^2)^{-1}dz=\pi(1+|v_0|^2)^{-1/2}$. Equation (11) gives
\[
 \rho^{(3)}(0,u,w)
 =\frac{\pi s^3}{2Z A_\triangle}\,
 \Psi(u,w),\qquad
 \Psi(u,w)=\int_{I^3}
      \frac{d\mathbf t}{\sqrt{1+|v_0(\mathbf t;u,w)|^2}}.
 \tag{21}
\]
The factor $\Psi$ is not constant. For a fixed noncollinear pair $u,w$ and $c>0$,
$v_0(\mathbf t;cu,cw)=c^{-1}v_0(\mathbf t;u,w)$. Except on the null set $t_1=t_2=t_3$, the corresponding integrand tends to zero as $c\downarrow0$. It is bounded by one, so dominated convergence gives
\[
 \Psi(cu,cw)\longrightarrow0,
 \qquad
 A_\triangle(0,cu,cw)\rho^{(3)}(0,cu,cw)\longrightarrow0.
 \tag{22}
\]
Moreover, $\Psi(cu,cw)$ is strictly increasing in $c$, because the integrand is strictly increasing outside that null set. The formulas in (21) are continuous on the noncollinear domain: on a neighbourhood of any such pair, the linear inverse defining $v_0$ depends continuously on $(u,w)$, and bounded convergence applies to the integrand. Thus almost-everywhere equality to $a_3/A_\triangle$ on the open domain $E_3(M)$ would imply pointwise equality there, in conflict with (22). The third required identity fails as well.

### 8. A class-specific obstruction to a pair-law repair

The failure at order two is not confined to the particular amplitude in (5).

**Proposition 2.** Consider a law constructed by first sampling $W\in\mathbb R^3$ with a radial Lebesgue probability density $q(v)=q_0(|v|)$, and then a uniform phase modulo a finite period $T(|W|)$. Conditional on these marks take a Poisson process with intensity
\[
 a(|W|)\sum_{k\in\mathbb Z}
 h(W\cdot x-B-kT(|W|)),
\]
where $h=\mathbf1_I$, $a(r)\geq0$ is finite, and
$T(r)\geq2+Lr$ for some $L>M>0$. Assume its full pair density is finite at some distance $d_0\in(0,M)$. Then
\[
 \lim_{d\downarrow0}d\rho^{(2)}(d)=0.
 \tag{23}
\]
Consequently no such law can have the full pair density $a_2/d$ with $a_2>0$ almost everywhere throughout $(0,M)$.

**Proof.** The same phase calculation and exclusion of distinct slab indices apply for $d<M$. Define the nonnegative radial weight
\[
 \omega(r)=\frac{q_0(r)a(r)^2r^2}{T(r)}.
\]
The overlap integral of two translates of $h$ is $(1-|t|)_+$. Polar integration with the polar axis along the separation gives
\[
 \rho^{(2)}(d)=\int_0^\infty\omega(r)H(dr)\,dr,
\quad
 H(z)=\begin{cases}
 4\pi(1-z/2),&0\leq z\leq1,\\
 2\pi/z,&z\geq1.
 \end{cases}
 \tag{24}
\]
For every fixed finite $r$, the function $d\mapsto dH(dr)$ is nondecreasing: it equals $4\pi d-2\pi r d^2$ before $dr=1$, where its derivative is $4\pi(1-rd)\geq0$, and equals $2\pi/r$ thereafter. For $r=0$ it is $4\pi d$. In all cases it tends to zero as $d\downarrow0$.

For $0<d\leq d_0$, the integrand $\omega(r)dH(dr)$ is bounded by the integrable function $\omega(r)d_0H(d_0r)$, because $\rho^{(2)}(d_0)<\infty$. Dominated convergence proves (23), and also shows that the pair density is finite at all $d\in(0,d_0]$.

If the reciprocal law held almost everywhere on $(0,M)$, one could choose $d_0$ from its finite full-measure set and a sequence $d_j\downarrow0$ from that set. The left side of (23) along the sequence would equal $a_2$, a contradiction. For a radial density, almost-everywhere equality in the spatial pair domain implies almost-everywhere equality in $d$ by polar integration. This proves the proposition. $\square$

This obstruction assumes the particular single, radial, finite-thickness, periodic slab construction with the stated separation. It is **not** a general non-existence result for S1, or for Cox processes. It does not justify altering the active target. In particular, it says nothing about all random intensity fields or all geometrical carriers.
